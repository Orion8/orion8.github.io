## Verified structure

The revised book and Korean edition use five parts and nine chapters.

1. Part I — Introduction: The Problem of Cooperation
2. Part II — The Emergence of Cooperation: the computer tournaments and chronology
3. Part III — Cooperation Without Friendship or Foresight: World War I trenches and biology
4. Part IV — Advice for Participants and Reformers: choosing effectively and promoting cooperation
5. Part V — Conclusions: social structure and the robustness of reciprocity

## Core claims used in the infographic

- A one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma rewards individual defection even though mutual cooperation would make both players better off.
- Repetition changes the strategic landscape when the same participants may meet again and the future is not fixed in advance.
- TIT FOR TAT cooperates first, then copies the other player's previous move.
- Its tournament success depended on four linked qualities: nice, provocable/retaliatory, forgiving, and clear.
- Reciprocity is not universally optimal. Its stability requires a sufficiently long shadow of the future.
- Cooperation can gain an initial foothold through clusters of reciprocators; once established, reciprocal cooperation can resist invasion.
- Axelrod and Hamilton separate three questions: robustness, stability, and initial viability.
- Applications in the book include World War I live-and-let-live systems and biological cooperation.
