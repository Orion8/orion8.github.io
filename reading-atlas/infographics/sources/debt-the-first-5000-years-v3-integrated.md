# Debt: The First 5,000 Years — V3 integrated source note

## Edition and title

- Original: *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* (David Graeber, 2011).
- Korean display title used by Reading Atlas: **부채, 그 첫 5,000년**.

2. The Myth of Barter

For infographic readability, the book's movement is shown as:

- **Chs. 1–7: concepts and moral relations** — barter myth, primordial debt, violence, exchange, hierarchy, honor.
- **Chs. 8–12: a 5,000-year chronology** — alternating eras of credit systems and bullion/coin money.

## Core question

What happens when a moral obligation between people becomes a precisely quantified debt enforceable by institutions and violence?

## Core arguments and model

1. **Credit and debt precede coinage.** The textbook sequence “barter → money → credit” is not supported by the historical and anthropological record.
2. **Debt is a quantified obligation.** An obligation becomes debt when it can be measured, transferred, and enforced.
3. **Three moral logics coexist:** everyday communism (“from each according to ability”), exchange (balanced reciprocity), and hierarchy (unequal but patterned obligation).
4. **Violence makes people and obligations calculable.** Conquest, slavery, patriarchy, and state power repeatedly turn social relations into prices and debts.
5. **History cycles between credit and bullion.** Credit-money eras often rely on trust and institutions; coin/bullion eras are repeatedly linked to war, taxation, armies, and slavery.

## Historical rhythm

- Early agrarian empires: account money and credit.
- Axial Age (800 BC–600 AD): coinage, armies, markets, slavery.
- Middle Ages (600–1450): renewed credit systems and moral regulation.
- Capitalist empires (1450–1971): bullion, conquest, public and private debt.
- Since 1971: fiat money and a still-unsettled credit order.

## Perspectives to learn

1. Money is a social and political relation, not only a neutral medium.
2. “Paying one's debts” is a moral claim whose force depends on power.
3. Markets and states develop together rather than as pure opposites.
4. Debt cancellation and jubilees recur because unlimited claims can destroy social order.
5. The book is intentionally revisionist; scholarly reviews praise its scope while questioning some origin stories and generalizations.

## Reading questions

1. Who has the power to define and enforce the debt?
2. What changed when an obligation became a number?
3. Is the relationship governed by communism, exchange, or hierarchy?
4. Does the historical evidence support a credit era or a bullion era?
