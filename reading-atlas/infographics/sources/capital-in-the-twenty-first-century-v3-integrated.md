# Le Capital au XXIe siècle / Capital in the Twenty-First Century

## Edition and title

- Original French edition: *Le Capital au XXIe siècle* (2013), Thomas Piketty.
- English edition: *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (Belknap Press of Harvard University Press, 2014), translated by Arthur Goldhammer.
- Korean edition: 《21세기 자본》, 토마 피케티, 장경덕 외 옮김, 글항아리 (2014).

## Verified structure

The book is organized as an introduction, four parts with sixteen chapters, and a conclusion.

1. **Part One — Income and Capital** (chapters 1–2): national income, output, and long-run growth.
2. **Part Two — The Dynamics of the Capital/Income Ratio** (chapters 3–6): the changing forms of capital, Europe and the New World, the long-run capital/income ratio, and the capital–labor split.
3. **Part Three — The Structure of Inequality** (chapters 7–12): concentration, labor-income inequality, capital ownership, inheritance, and global wealth inequality.
4. **Part Four — Regulating Capital in the Twenty-First Century** (chapters 13–16): the social state, progressive income taxation, a global capital tax, and public debt.

## Core analytical model

- **First fundamental law:** `α = r × β`, where `α` is capital's share of national income, `r` the average return on capital, and `β` the capital/income ratio.
- **Second fundamental law:** `β = s / g` in the long run, where `s` is the savings rate and `g` the growth rate. This is a steady-state relationship with conditions, not a short-run mechanical rule.
- **Divergence force:** when `r > g`, inherited wealth can grow faster than income and output, strengthening wealth concentration. Piketty explicitly warns that this relation alone is not a deterministic, all-purpose explanation; institutions, shocks, saving behavior, taxation, and politics matter.

## Five perspectives to learn

1. Treat distribution as a central economic outcome, not an afterthought.
2. Separate accounting identities from causal mechanisms and policy claims.
3. Read inequality over centuries, using tax, estate, income, and national-balance-sheet data together.
4. Distinguish labor-income inequality from wealth and inheritance inequality.
5. See taxation and social institutions as historically contingent political choices.

## Reading questions

- Which claim is an identity, which is a long-run model, and which is an empirical tendency?
- How does slower growth change the relative weight of accumulated and inherited wealth?
- Where do wars, inflation, taxation, and institutions interrupt the simple `r > g` story?
- What data would be needed to evaluate a progressive capital tax fairly and internationally?
