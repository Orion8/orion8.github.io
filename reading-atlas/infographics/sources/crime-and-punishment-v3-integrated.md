# Crime and Punishment — V3 integrated infographic

Original title: Преступление и наказание. Fyodor Dostoyevsky, 1866.

## Sources and structural baseline

- Primary novel, Constance Garnett translation: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/2554/2554-h/2554-h.htm
- Penguin Classics publisher description: https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/286337/crime-and-punishment-by-fyodor-dostoyevsky-translated-with-an-introduction-and-notes-by-david-mcduff-cover-illustrated-by-coralie-bickford-smith/9780140449136/

The novel contains six parts and an epilogue (the epilogue has two chapters). The infographic clearly labels the grouped descriptions of Part I, Parts II–III, Parts IV–VI and the epilogue as a reader's guide, not original part titles. The main story begins in unusually hot July in St Petersburg; the epilogue moves to Siberia. Main-story winter imagery was corrected during image review. The epilogue's prison and river illustrations are interpretive, not documentary views of an exact location or moment.

Part III, Chapter V presents and interrogates Raskolnikov's extraordinary-person argument. The illustrated sequence from exception-belief through transgression, anxiety/isolation and possible responsibility is a reading lens, not a scientific or author-stated formal model. The image explicitly distinguishes the protagonist's theory from the author's verdict. Redemption is presented as a possibility, not a claim that confession or legal punishment instantly produces inner change. The four character captions are concise interpretive lenses, not complete character descriptions.

## Production

Built-in image generation, independently composed Korean and English, with all captions and illustrations embedded in each raster from the outset. Targeted image edits corrected seasonal/location mistakes and removed rounded English caption backgrounds. No web text overlay, external font, SVG text or shared language-switching background. Prompts: five numbered sections for central moral question, six-part structural guide, self-justification under pressure, four character relationships and paired reading questions; bright ivory integrated editorial grid, meaningful nineteenth-century illustrations and dark sharp text.
